The rupture of reality filters

Psychosis is an episodic neurobiological syndrome characterized by a disruption in the brain's ability to distinguish internal mental events from external physical reality. Every millisecond, the human thalamus filters out billions of pieces of sensory noise. In psychosis, that protective filter fractures: internal thoughts echo as audible voices, everyday objects blaze with urgent cosmic meaning, and the boundary between the self and the universe dissolves.

Psychosis is a temporary breakdown of sensory gating and reality calibration, not a moral failure or personal choice.

Chapter 2 of 7, Core domains

The psychotic spectrum.

Psychosis spans hallucinations, delusions, thought disorganization, and negative symptoms across multiple clinical conditions.

Domain 1, Positive symptoms

Perceptual additions.

Phenomena that invade consciousness with vivid, undeniable reality. The brain's auditory cortex lights up identically to actual spoken sound waves.

  • Auditory hallucinationsHearing voices, whispering frequencies, or ambient music with total acoustic presence.
  • Delusions of referenceNeutral events (a passing car, a bird landing) feel cosmically intentional and targeted.
  • Persecutory convictionThe terrifying belief of being watched, tracked, or threatened by unseen external agencies.
  • Thought insertion and broadcastingThe subjective experience that private thoughts are being placed in the head or broadcast aloud.
TakeawayReal to the senses.
Positive symptoms: additions to experience

'Positive' symptoms refer to excess sensory or cognitive phenomena added to consciousness: auditory hallucinations (hearing voices that sound 100% external and real), visual distortions, delusions of reference (believing headlights, radio lyrics, or billboards contain personal coded instructions), and persecutory delusions (the conviction that external forces are monitoring or targeting you).

Hallucinations sound completely physical to the person experiencing them; arguing does not change sensory perception.

Domain 2, Negative and cognitive

Cognitive disorganization.

The fragmentation of working memory, motivation, and conceptual bridges, leaving the person feeling marooned in internal silence.

  • Thought blockingA sudden, inexplicable erasure of conscious thought mid-sentence; the mental bridge dissolves.
  • Avolition and lethargySevere depletion of motivational dopamine; basic tasks feel like moving through heavy concrete.
  • Conceptual slippageAssociations drift off topic through loose metaphors that make intuitive sense only to the speaker.
  • Affective maskingExpressive flattening where facial cues freeze, even while intense terror or confusion churns inside.
TakeawayThe quiet depletion.
Negative and cognitive symptoms: losses and disorganization

'Negative' symptoms represent the draining of normal psychological function: avolition (inability to initiate goal-directed action), alogia (poverty of speech), affective flattening (diminished emotional expression despite internal distress), and social withdrawal. Cognitive symptoms include thought blocking (where a train of thought abruptly vanishes into a void) and conceptual loosening.

Negative symptoms are often the most functionally impairing long-term aspects and require compassionate structure, not blame.

Chapter 3 of 7, Neurocomputation

The salience engine.

Understanding why delusions feel so undeniably persuasive:

Dopamine hyper-drive. Mesolimbic excess creates hyper-meaning; mesocortical deficit drives negative symptoms.

Coordinated care. Early intervention in the first episode reduces hospitalization recurrence by 50%+.

TakeawayBiology behind the belief.
The neurocomputational engine: aberrant salience

Hyper-dopaminergic firing in the mesolimbic tract causes neutral stimuli to radiate with intense, unearned emotional importance ('aberrant salience'). When a mundane street sign or a random word triggers the brain's internal alarm bell, the conscious mind is forced to invent a narrative, a delusion, to justify why that object feels so earth-shatteringly vital.

Delusions are the mind's rational attempt to explain an irrational, hyper-salient neurochemical signal.

Kapur's salience model

Dopamine mediates the conversion of neutral neural representations into attractive or aversive targets of attention. In psychosis, dopamine neurons fire randomly and out of synchrony with reality. This creates a state of pervasive hyper-meaning: every coincidence feels like prophecy, every glance feels like surveillance. Antipsychotics work by dampening this excessive salience, turning down the volume of the signal so reality can re-stabilize.

Antipsychotics do not erase beliefs overnight; they extinguish the emotional voltage that makes the belief feel so desperately important.

Chapter 4 of 7, Somatic waves

Decode the signal storm.

Open any symptom to inspect its underlying neurological mechanism:

Auditory perceptions

Internal speech loops (the phonological buffer) fire spontaneously without efference copy tags, causing your own thoughts to sound like an external voice speaking in the room.

Loss of self-monitoring efference copy causes internal thoughts to sound external.

Ideas of reference

Street signs, car license plates, or radio songs feel intentionally coded specifically for you. The brain's pattern-recognition software operates in overdrive.

Hyperactive pattern recognition makes neutral coincidences feel intentional.

Thought broadcasting

The boundary between internal subjective thought and external space dissolves. You feel convinced that people on the subway or in the room can overhear your mind.

Ego-boundary dissolution creates the sensation of transparent cognition.

Sensory gating deficit

The thalamic reticular nucleus fails to filter background sensory inputs. Fluorescent lights flicker deafeningly, refrigerator hums roar, and multiple conversations bleed together.

Sensory gating failure overwhelms working memory with raw stimuli.

Paranoid hyper-vigilance

The amygdala is locked in chronic emergency overdrive. Heart rate climbs, pupils dilate, and every shadow looks like a potential ambush.

Extreme threat detection is an involuntary neurochemical panic state.

Cognitive fragmentation

Associations break apart (loosening of associations). Thoughts derail into unexpected tangents, making sustained conversation difficult.

Prefrontal cortical disconnect disrupts semantic continuity.

Affective masking

Facial muscles and vocal pitch become flat and impassive (flat affect) even while the person is experiencing intense terror or confusion inside.

Flat affect is motor-expressive, not an absence of internal emotion.

Catatonic freezing

Motor cortex pathways freeze or fluctuate into repetitive purposeless motor pacing. Sudden paralysis where the body cannot initiate the next step.

Catatonia responds rapidly to GABAergic intervention and medical care.

TakeawayReal brain chemistry.

Critical: the three rules of psychosis support
  • Reduce stimuliTurn down bright lights, lower noise, and speak in slow, calm sentences.
  • Emergency protocolIf safety is at risk, contact a mobile crisis team or dial 988.
TakeawayConnect to the feeling, ensure physical safety, and avoid argumentative confrontation.
How to support someone in psychosis

Directly confronting or mocking a delusion never works: to them, the threat is as real as gravity. Denying their perception creates isolation and fear. Conversely, agreeing with the delusion confirms false danger. The gold standard: validate the emotion, not the delusion. Say: 'I don't hear the voices, but I can see how terrified you are right now. You are safe here with me, and we are going to get through this together.'

Connect to the feeling, ensure physical safety, and avoid argumentative confrontation.

Chapter 6 of 7, Recovery science

The recovery trajectory.

Evidence-based pillars that restore cognitive clarity and stability:

TakeawayRecovery is attainable.
The critical window: early psychosis intervention

The first episode of psychosis (FEP) is a pivotal neurodevelopmental junction. Coordinated Specialty Care (CSC) programs, combining low-dose antipsychotic pharmacotherapy, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Psychosis (CBTp), family education, and supported education and employment, produce extraordinary long-term recovery rates. The shorter the Duration of Untreated Psychosis (DUP), the better the cognitive preservation.

Psychosis is treatable. Early specialized intervention restores independence, relationships, and meaningful life paths.

The grounded anchor: sensory stabilization

When perceptual filters fail, tactile, physical sensory anchors are the most reliable tool to tether the mind back to consensual reality. The 5-4-3-2-1 technique anchors the sensory gates to undeniable physical objects: 5 things you can see right now, 4 textures you can touch, 3 ambient room sounds, 2 physical breaths, and 1 core undeniable truth. Physical touch, solid weight, and steady breath provide the anchor when internal perception feels unmoored.
