Clifford Whittingham Beers, 1908

A Mind That Found Itself.

An autobiography of the years 1900 to 1903, most of them spent in psychiatric institutions, written by the man who lived them and then set out to reform how people with mental illness are treated.

Chapter I is the author’s statement of why the book exists. It was first published on March 16, 1908.

“It is fit to remain in literature as a classic account ‘from within’ of an insane person’s psychology.”William James, letter to Beers, November 10, 1907

Beers wrote in the clinical language of 1908. Words such as “insane” and “insanity” are his, quoted as written.

Chapter I, A mental civil war

Another self.

Beers frames the book as more than an autobiography. In part it is a biography of a different self, one that ruled him from his twenty-fourth to his twenty-sixth year, unlike who he had been before or became after.

“The history of a mental civil war, which I fought single-handed on a battle-field that lay within the compass of my skull.”Chapter I, page 1
  • The Army of Unreason
  • A triumphant Reason

He describes the illness as an Army of Unreason, made of “cunning and treacherous thoughts,” attacking a bewildered mind until Reason “interposed a superior strategy.”

Chapter I, Why tell it

For what purpose was my life spared?

Beers survived what looked like a fatal illness and asked what his life had been spared for. The book is part of his answer: he tells the story because it seems his “plain duty” to do so.

“Until some one tells just such a story as mine and tells it sanely, needless abuse of helpless thousands will continue.”Chapter I, page 2

He grants that most patients of his day were treated with real consideration. His target is the minority, thousands of people, still subjected to brutal abuse, and the lack of direct proof that it was happening. As a former patient, he claims the right to expose those methods and offers a remedy: a campaign for reform.

Chapter I, The scale in 1908

A million people in this country.

Beers argues that the subject is economic as well as humanitarian. These are his own estimates, as he gave them in 1908.

  • 200,000People in asylums, hospitals, sanatoriums, and homes in the United States.
  • 5Relatives and friends he assumed each person had who cared about their welfare.
  • 1 in 80Together, about a million people in the country directly or indirectly affected.
  • 150,000Mental incompetents he counted in Great Britain, with a like number in France and Germany.

He adds that anyone among the remaining seventy-nine million might one day be forced to join this “army of distress.” Few subjects, he writes, are so misunderstood or so willingly ignored.

Chapter I, A question of research

Why should it remain incurable?

Beers places mental illness beside the great infectious diseases of his time, each at a different stage of being beaten:

  • Small-poxConquered.
  • DiphtheriaDoomed.
  • Yellow feverConfined within limits.
  • TuberculosisPartially controlled and not infrequently cured.
  • InsanityStill on the list of incurable diseases. Why?

Recognized authorities, he notes, predicted most forms would in time prove curable, but only after years of systematic scientific research that had barely begun. He hoped the twentieth century would be remembered as the one in which the cause and cure of most mental diseases were found.

What the book is for.

TakeawayDoing to the insane as the sane would be done by is the essence of Non-Restraint.
What followed

The campaign he proposed.

  1. 1March 16, 1908The first edition is published, proposing a permanent National Committee modeled on the work against tuberculosis.
  2. 2May 6, 1908The Connecticut Society for Mental Hygiene is formally founded, the first state society, to work the problem out in one state first.
  3. 3By May 1, 1910The National Committee for Mental Hygiene is organized, with the aim of helping found an allied Society for Mental Hygiene in each state.
The National Committee’s chief objects, as listed in 1910

(a) to work for the protection of the mental health of the public; (b) to help raise the standards of care for those threatened with nervous or mental disorder or actually ill; (c) to promote the study of mental disorders in all their forms and relations and to disseminate knowledge concerning their causes, treatment and prevention; (d) to obtain from every source reliable data regarding conditions and methods of dealing with mental disorders; (e) to enlist the aid of the Federal Government so far as may seem desirable; (f) to co-ordinate the work of existing agencies and to help organize in each State in the Union an allied but independent Society for Mental Hygiene.

From the Postscript, dated May 1, 1910.
